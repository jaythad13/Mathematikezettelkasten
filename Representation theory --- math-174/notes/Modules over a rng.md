Let $R$ be a (possibly non-unital, possibly non-commutative) ring. We call this a rng and reserve ring for unital rings.

##### _definition:_ modules over a rng

A **(left) module** over $R$ is an abelian group $M$ with a rng homomorphism $R \to \operatorname{End}_{\mathsf{Ab}} M$.

Note, $\operatorname{End}_{\mathsf{Ab}} M$ is a (unital, possibly non-commutative) ring with addition given by addition of endomorphisms and multiplication given by composition.

If $R$ is a (possibly non-commutative) ring then we require $R \to \operatorname{End}_{\mathsf{Ab}} M$ to be a ring homomorphism (that is, it should preserve identity).

We write this as an action of $R$ — if the structure morphism is $\varphi$, then $\varphi(r)m$ is written $r \cdot m$.

The following example is very close to what we do in representation theory.

##### _example:_ a representation theoretic example

Let $T$ be a linear operator on a (finite dimensional) vector space $V$ (over some field $\mathbb{F}$). Then [[Linear algebra done right --- ladr/notes/Polynomials in an operator|we have seen]] that $\mathbb{F}[z]$ acts on $V$ through $p(z) \cdot v = p(T) \cdot v$. In particular, we have a ring homomorphism $\mathbb{F}[z] \to \mathbb{F}[T] \to \operatorname{End} V$.

In fact, every action $\mathbb{F}[z] \circlearrowright V$ must come from $z$ acting by a linear map $T \in \operatorname{End} V$. The structure theorem for PIDs then implies that we can break down this action into invariant pieces, and so we can write $T$ in canonical forms.

The following example is literally all of representation theory pretty much.

##### _example:_ modules over group rings, or the fundamental representation-theoretic example, or permutation modules

Suppose $G$ is a finite group acting on a finite set $X$. Then the [[Abstract algebra --- math-171/notes/Rings#_example _ group rings|group ring]] $\mathbb{F}[G]$ acts on the free $\mathbb{F}$-vector space generated by $X$. The action is just the $\mathbb{F}$-linear extension of the action of $G$ on $X$. That is, $\mathbb{F}^{X}$ is an $\mathbb{F}[G]$-module. 

If you think of $\mathbb{F}^{X}$ as having basis $X$, this is easy. If you think of $\mathbb{F}^X$ as $\mathbb{F}$-valued functions on $X$, then $(g \cdot f)(x) = f(g^{-1} \cdot x)$ and extend $\mathbb{F}$-linearly in the action. 

Note, these are the same, even though it takes a second to figure it out. Think of the example of a function on the vertices of $C_{4}$ (the cyclic graph with $4$ vertices) and how you figure out how to move the values of the function.

We call this the **permutation module** structure on $\mathbb{F}^X$ (for a $G$-set $X$).

---

##### _example:_ random walks on finite groups

Consider a random walk on $C_{4}$ where you stay in place half the time, rotate one way a quarter of the time, and rotate the other way a quarter of the time. Then this corresponds to computing $1 / 2 + r / 4 + r^{-1} / 4$ in the group ring $\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{Z} / 4]$ or even $\mathbb{Z}[D_{2 \times 4}]$.
