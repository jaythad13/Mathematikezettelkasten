\begin{problem}[1.]
	Prove that if $f : \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $\partial f(t, x) / \partial x$ is a bounded function, then $f$ is Lipschitz in its second variable.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
	For each $t \in \mathbb{R}$, let $f_{t} : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be the function with  $f_{t}(x) = f(t, x)$. By definition, ${\partial f}/{\partial x} = f_{t}'$. Thus, $f_{t}$ has bounded derivative. In particular, let $\lvert f_{t}' \rvert < \lambda$.

	By the mean value theorem, for any $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$, there is some $z \in [x, y]$ such that $f'_{t}(z) = ( f_{t}(y) - f_{t}(x) ) / ( y - x )$. Thus, any difference quotient has absolute value bounded by $\lambda$. Thus, $f_{t}$ is Lipschitz ---
	\[
		\lvert f_{t}(y) - f_{t}(x) \rvert < \lambda \lvert y - x \rvert.
	\]

\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[2.]
	Prove that $f(t, x) = \sin{(t + x)}$ is Lipschitz and that $g(t, x) = e^{x} \sin{(t + x)}$ is not Lipschitz.
\end{problem}g

\begin{solution}
	Since $z \mapsto \sin{z}$ is absolutely bounded, $f$ is an absolutely bounded function (in particular $\lvert f(t, x) \rvert \le 1$). Thus, $\lvert f(t_{2}, x_{2}) - f(t_{1}, x_{1}) \rvert < 3$ for all $(t_{2}, x_{2}), (t_{1}, x_{1}) \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$.

	To show $g$ is not Lipschitz, for each  $\lambda > 0$ we find two points $(t_{1}, x_{1}), (t_{2}, x_{2})$ distance $1$ apart such that $\lvert g(t_{2}, x_{2}) - g(t_{1}, x_{2}) \rvert > 2 \lambda$. In particular, for $\lambda > 0$, we choose $x_{1} > \log \lambda$ such that $x_{1} = 2 \pi n$. We choose $x_{2} = x_{1} + 1$ and $t_{2} = t_{1} = 0$. Then $g(t_{2}, x_{2}) = \sin{(1)} e^{x_{2}} > 2 \lambda / 2 > \lambda$. Further, $g(t_{1}, x_{1}) = \sin{(2 \pi n)} e^{x_{1}} = 0$. Thus, $g$ is not Lipschitz.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[3.]
	Let $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a closed bounded interval and $C(K)$ be the set of continuous function $K \to \mathbb{R}$. For $f, g \in C(K)$ define $d(f, g) = \max$. Prove that $C(K), d$ is a complete metric space.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
	Suppose $(f_{n} )_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a Cauchy sequence in $C(K)$. Then we can define a sensible function $f : K \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by $f(x) = \lim_{ n \to \infty } f_{n}(x)$ since the reals are complete. This follows since the maximum distance between $f_{n}(x)$ and $f_{m}(x)$ being bounded by $\varepsilon$ means this bound holds at each $x \in K$, and thus, each $(f_{n}(x))_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a Cauchy sequence.

It remains to show that $f$ is continuous and $f_{n} \to f$. 

The first will follow by the strength of the convergence. We can show that $f$ is continuous is true by picking $N$ large enough and $x$ close enough to $x_{0}$ at each $x_{0}$. 

Now we show that the point-wise limit $f$ is really the limit of the $f_{n}$ under the $\sup$ metric.

First we show that $f_{n} \to f$ locally — for any $\varepsilon > 0$, around each $x \in K$ there is an open ball $B_{\delta}(x)$ with its image under $f - f_{n}$ less than $\varepsilon$ away from $0$. This follows by choosing $\delta$ small enough that $f$ is close to $f(x)$ and $f_{n}$ is close to $f_{n}(x)$, and $n$ large enough that $f_{n}(x)$ is close to $f(x)$. (Technically, to avoid $\delta$ depending on $n$, we use a fourth term in the triangle inequality to show with $N$ big enough that the Cauchy condition holds, so that we can have a uniform $\delta$ to compare against).

But then these $B_{\delta}(x)$ form an open cover of compact $K$, and must reduce to a finite sub-cover. Then by picking the largest $n$ required in any of the $\delta$-balls for $f$ and $f_{n}$ to be close, we have $f$ and $f_{n}$ uniformly close on $K$. That is, $f_{n} \to f$.
\end{solution}
